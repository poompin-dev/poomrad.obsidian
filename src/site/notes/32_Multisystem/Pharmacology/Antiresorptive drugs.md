> [!summary]
> Antiresorptive drugs suppress osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and reduce fragility-fracture or skeletal-event risk; the key imaging associations are [[30_MSK/Bones/Trauma/Special fracture/Atypical femoral fracture\|atypical femoral fracture]], medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, vertebral fractures after denosumab interruption and treatment-related metaphyseal bands in children.

---
## Main drugs

### Bisphosphonates
- **Oral:** alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate
- **Intravenous:** zoledronate, pamidronate
- Bind bone mineral → taken up by osteoclasts → osteoclast inhibition/apoptosis
- Long skeletal half-life → effect persists after stopping

### Denosumab
- Monoclonal antibody against receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL)
- Suppresses osteoclast formation and function
- Unlike bisphosphonates, it does not bind bone; its antiresorptive effect reverses when dosing lapses.
- Delaying or stopping treatment without subsequent antiresorptive therapy can cause rapid bone loss and multiple vertebral fractures, so a drug holiday is not recommended

### Other antiresorptives
Raloxifene, oestrogen and calcitonin also reduce bone resorption; this note focuses on bisphosphonates and denosumab because they account for the principal MSK imaging associations.

## Clinical uses
- [[30_MSK/Bones/Metabolic/Osteoporosis/Osteoporosis\|Osteoporosis]]
- [[30_MSK/Bones/Bony lesions/Lytic lesions/Malignant/Multiple myeloma\|Multiple myeloma]]
- Bone metastases
- Hypercalcaemia of malignancy
- [[30_MSK/Bones/Bony lesions/Lytic lesions/Benign/Giant cell tumour\|Giant cell tumour]] of bone: denosumab for unresectable disease or when surgical resection is likely to cause severe morbidity; preoperative use before curettage is selected and controversial because viable neoplastic stromal cells may remain within treatment-induced bone
- Selected paediatric bone-fragility disorders, including osteogenesis imperfecta: commonly intravenous bisphosphonate therapy under specialist care
- [[Paget disease of bone\|Paget disease of bone]]: potent bisphosphonate therapy
- Bone loss associated with systemic glucocorticoids, androgen-deprivation therapy or aromatase-inhibitor therapy in patients at sufficient fracture risk

## Complications

### [[30_MSK/Bones/Trauma/Special fracture/Atypical femoral fracture\|Atypical femoral fracture]]
- Usually arises from the lateral cortex between the lesser trochanter and supracondylar flare, with a transverse or short-oblique fracture line, focal lateral cortical thickening or beaking, minimal comminution and little or no trauma.
- Classically associated with prolonged bisphosphonate exposure, but also reported with denosumab.
- Prodromal thigh or groin pain and bilateral involvement are recognised.

> [!warning]
> When an atypical femoral fracture or lateral cortical stress reaction is identified, image the full length of both femora to assess incomplete or contralateral disease.

### Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw
- Clinical case definition: current or previous exposure to an antiresorptive drug, alone or with an immune modulator or antiangiogenic drug; exposed bone or bone probed through a fistula in the maxillofacial region for more than 8 weeks; and no jaw irradiation or jaw metastasis.
- The mandible is affected more often than the maxilla. Dentoalveolar surgery, particularly extraction, is the most common identifiable local precipitating event, often in the setting of pre-existing inflammatory dental disease.
- Imaging may show a non-healing extraction socket, mixed sclerosis and osteolysis, sequestration, periosteal new bone or cortical disruption, but imaging alone does not establish MRONJ.
- Risk is substantially higher with oncology-dose antiresorptive therapy than with osteoporosis-dose therapy.

### Denosumab interruption
- New acute or subacute vertebral compression fractures, often multiple, after a delayed or stopped denosumab course should prompt correlation with the date of the last dose and whether sequential antiresorptive therapy was given.
- Rebound hypercalcaemia may occur after discontinuation in patients with [[30_MSK/Bones/Bony lesions/Lytic lesions/Benign/Giant cell tumour\|giant cell tumour]] of bone and in patients with a growing skeleton.

### Other clinically important adverse effects
- Hypocalcaemia: particularly important with denosumab in advanced chronic kidney disease or chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder.
- Acute phase reaction: fever, myalgia and arthralgia in the days after a first intravenous zoledronate infusion.
- Renal restrictions are drug- and indication-specific: osteoporosis-dose zoledronate is contraindicated when creatinine clearance is below 35 mL/min or in acute renal impairment. Denosumab is not renally cleared, but advanced chronic kidney disease and dialysis substantially increase its hypocalcaemia risk.
- Oral bisphosphonate: oesophagitis and oesophageal ulceration, hence upright dosing with water.

## Radiology pearls

### Paediatric bisphosphonate therapy
- **Metaphyseal sclerotic bands** or **zebra lines**: multiple dense transverse bands parallel to active physes in children receiving cyclical intravenous bisphosphonate therapy, classically pamidronate for osteogenesis imperfecta. The number and spacing may reflect treatment cycles and intervening growth; the bands migrate towards the diaphysis and remodel with growth.
- Vertebral **picture-frame** or **bone-within-bone** sclerosis may occur during or after bisphosphonate therapy in the growing skeleton.

> [!tip]
> Recognise zebra lines as a treatment marker: correlate their number and spacing with the infusion history rather than mistaking them for a new sclerosing disorder.

### Denosumab-treated giant cell tumour
- Progressive re-ossification with a peripheral sclerotic rim and cortical remodelling on CT, and falling T2 signal.
- Treatment-induced peripheral and intralesional bone can make the original tumour boundary difficult to recognise during curettage; radiological-pathological correlation is required because viable neoplastic stromal cells may persist. See [[30_MSK/Bones/Bony lesions/Lytic lesions/Benign/Giant cell tumour\|Giant cell tumour]].
- New or recurrent osteolysis after denosumab cessation suggests tumour reactivation or recurrence; recurrence is possible because denosumab suppresses osteoclast-mediated tumour effects without reliably eradicating the neoplastic stromal-cell population.
