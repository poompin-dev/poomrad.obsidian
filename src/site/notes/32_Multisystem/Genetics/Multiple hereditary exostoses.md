> [!summary]
> Autosomal dominant skeletal dysplasia characterised by multiple [[30_MSK/Bones/Bony lesions/Lytic lesions/Benign/Osteochondroma\|osteochondromas]], with deformity from disturbed physeal growth and a low lifetime risk of secondary peripheral [[30_MSK/Bones/Bony lesions/Lytic lesions/Malignant/Chondrosarcoma\|chondrosarcoma]].

---
## Genetics and clinical pattern
- Osteochondromas develop in childhood, increase in number and size during skeletal growth, and usually stop enlarging at skeletal maturity.
- Common manifestations are short stature, limb-length discrepancy, forearm bowing or shortening, and knee or ankle angular deformity.
- Lesions may be asymptomatic or cause pain, restricted motion, tendon irritation, bursal formation, fracture, or neurovascular compression.

---
## Distribution
- Arise from the juxtaphyseal metaphyses of endochondral bones, most conspicuously around the knee, proximal humerus and forearm.
- Spinal involvement is often clinically occult and is not rare on screening MRI; symptomatic spinal-canal compromise is uncommon. The calvaria is not typically involved.

---
## Imaging

### Radiograph
- Multiple sessile or pedunculated cartilage-capped exostoses with uninterrupted cortical and medullary continuity with the parent bone.
- Document associated bowing, shortening, physeal disturbance and joint malalignment.

### CT
- Defines cortical and medullary continuity at complex sites and shows mineralisation, cortical destruction and the relation to adjacent neurovascular structures.

### MRI
- Best modality for cartilage-cap measurement, a suspected soft tissue mass, marrow or soft tissue extension, and neurovascular or spinal complications.
- Measure the maximum cap thickness perpendicular to the bone-cartilage interface, excluding crevices and any overlying bursa. A cap **≥2 cm in a skeletally mature patient** strongly suggests secondary chondrosarcoma; this adult threshold must not be applied to an immature skeleton.

> [!tip]
> The defining feature is cortical and medullary continuity with the parent bone; cap thickness is a risk discriminator.

---
## Complications
- Secondary peripheral [[30_MSK/Bones/Bony lesions/Lytic lesions/Malignant/Chondrosarcoma\|chondrosarcoma]] usually arises within the cartilage cap. Estimated lifetime risk is approximately **2–10%**, with large cohorts reporting about 3–5%; the pelvis and scapula are common transformation sites.
    - Suspicious features: new or increasing pain, growth after skeletal maturity, a thick or irregular cap, new soft tissue mass, cortical destruction, and adjacent marrow or soft tissue oedema.
- Mechanical tendon, muscle, nerve or vascular impingement.
- **Exostosis bursata:** Reactive bursal formation over a prominent lesion, which may mimic a thick cartilage cap.
- Rare [[30_MSK/Bones/Trauma/Atraumatic fracture/Pathologic fracture\|pathological fracture]], most often through a pedunculated stalk.

> [!warning]
> New pain or growth after skeletal maturity warrants targeted MRI and referral to a bone-tumour service when imaging is suspicious.
