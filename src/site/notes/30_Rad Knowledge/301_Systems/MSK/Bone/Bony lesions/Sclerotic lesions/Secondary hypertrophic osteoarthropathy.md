> [!summary]
> **Acquired [[30_Rad Knowledge/301_Systems/MSK/Bone/Bony lesions/Sclerotic lesions/Hypertrophic osteoarthropathy\|hypertrophic osteoarthropathy]] caused by an underlying disease**, classically an intrathoracic malignancy. The core pattern is **digital clubbing, painful arthropathy, and bilateral symmetric periostosis of the long bones**, predominantly involving the diaphyses and metaphyses while usually sparing the epiphyses.

---

## Terminology

- **Secondary hypertrophic osteoarthropathy (secondary HOA)** is the common acquired form of HOA.
- **Hypertrophic pulmonary osteoarthropathy (HPOA)** is the subset caused by **pulmonary or other intrathoracic disease**.
- HPOA is often used loosely for all secondary HOA, but this is inaccurate when the underlying cause is cardiac, gastrointestinal, or hepatobiliary.
- Secondary HOA is far more common than [[30_Rad Knowledge/301_Systems/MSK/Bone/Bony lesions/Sclerotic lesions/Primary hypertrophic osteoarthropathy\|primary HOA]].

---

## Pathophysiology

- The exact mechanism is incompletely understood.
- The leading model involves megakaryocytes and platelet clumps bypassing normal fragmentation in the pulmonary circulation:
	1. Megakaryocytes / platelets reach the systemic circulation.
	2. They lodge in distal extremity capillaries.
	3. Release of **VEGF, PDGF, and other growth factors** produces vascular, periosteal, and connective-tissue proliferation.
- Increased prostaglandin E2 and neurogenic mechanisms may also contribute.

---

## Causes

> [!tip]
> In an adult with new secondary HOA, **look for an intrathoracic malignancy first**, particularly lung carcinoma.

| Category | Important causes |
| --- | --- |
| **Malignant thoracic** | Primary lung carcinoma, pleural malignancy / mesothelioma, pulmonary or pleural metastases |
| **Nonmalignant pulmonary** | Bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, lung abscess, empyema, tuberculosis, pulmonary arteriovenous malformation |
| **Cardiac** | Cyanotic congenital heart disease, infective endocarditis, atrial myxoma |
| **Gastrointestinal** | Inflammatory bowel disease, GI malignancy, polyposis, achalasia |
| **Hepatobiliary** | Cirrhosis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, primary biliary cholangitis, biliary atresia, hepatobiliary malignancy |
| **Other** | Infected vascular graft, myelofibrosis, haematological malignancy |

## Clinical presentation

- Usually **acquired in adulthood**
	- Important paediatric exceptions include cyanotic congenital heart disease and cystic fibrosis.
- The syndrome may be complete or incomplete.
- Classic features:
	- **Digital clubbing**
	- **Deep aching bone pain** and tender swelling of the distal limbs
	- **Arthralgia, arthritis, or joint effusions**
- Symptoms may worsen with weight-bearing.
- Musculoskeletal manifestations can precede recognition of the underlying disease.

---

## Imaging

### Radiograph

- **Bilateral, symmetric periosteal new bone formation** without an associated destructive osseous lesion
- Predominantly affects the distal appendicular skeleton:
	- Tibia and fibula
	- Radius and ulna
	- Femur and humerus in more extensive disease
	- Short tubular bones may become involved
- Distribution usually progresses:
	1. **Diaphysis**
	2. **Metaphysis**
	3. Epiphyses are **typically spared**
- Morphology evolves with disease duration:
	- Early: thin, smooth, single-layer periostosis
	- Progressive: multilayered or **lamellated** periostosis
	- Chronic: thicker solid or irregular cortical new bone
- Associated soft-tissue swelling and digital clubbing

> [!tip]
> **Smooth or lamellated diaphyseal periostosis with epiphyseal sparing** favours secondary HOA. More exuberant, shaggy periostosis extending to the epiphyses favours [[30_Rad Knowledge/301_Systems/MSK/Bone/Bony lesions/Sclerotic lesions/Primary hypertrophic osteoarthropathy\|primary HOA]].

### Bone scintigraphy

- More sensitive than radiographs for early or subtle disease
- Shows diffuse, symmetric linear cortical uptake along the long bones
- Parallel cortical uptake produces the **[[70_Glossary/MSK/Double stripe sign\|double-stripe / tramline sign]]**
- Uptake may regress after successful treatment and can be used to assess response.

### CT and MRI

- Limb CT demonstrates periosteal new bone and cortical thickening.
- MRI may show periosteal elevation, adjacent soft-tissue oedema, and joint effusions.
- Cross-sectional imaging is usually more important for **identifying the underlying cause**, particularly CT of the chest.
- Symmetric periosteal uptake on FDG PET/CT may mimic osseous metastases; the cortical distribution and symmetry favour HOA.

---
## Practical work-up

1. Confirm that the pattern is **bilateral, symmetric periostosis** with clubbing or arthropathy.
2. Review previous imaging to establish onset and progression.
3. Search for an underlying cause:
	- **Chest imaging first** in a new adult presentation
	- Then targeted cardiac, gastrointestinal, and hepatobiliary evaluation according to the clinical context
4. If no secondary cause is found, reconsider primary HOA and other causes of diffuse periostosis.

---
## Management
- Treat the underlying disease.
- Periostosis, bone pain, and scintigraphic abnormalities may regress when the cause is successfully treated.
- Recurrence of HOA symptoms can indicate recurrence or progression of the underlying disease.
