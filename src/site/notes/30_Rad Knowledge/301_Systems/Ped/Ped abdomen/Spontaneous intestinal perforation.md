> [!info] Key points
> 
> - Distinct entity from NEC, not a variant
> - Focal full-thickness ischaemic perforation, typically **terminal ileum**, with **no surrounding inflammation or pneumatosis**
> - Almost exclusively **ELBW (<1000 g, <28/40)** infants
> - **Early onset**: usually first week of life (often day 3-7), before feeds are advanced
> - Strongly associated with **early indomethacin ± postnatal steroids**
> - **Pneumoperitoneum without pneumatosis or PVG** is the classic AXR pattern

---
## Epidemiology & risk factors
- Affects ~5% of ELBW infants
- Earlier onset than [[30_Rad Knowledge/301_Systems/Ped/Ped abdomen/Necrotising enterocolitis\|NEC]], often before enteral feeds established
- **Drug-related**: early postnatal indomethacin (PDA closure), postnatal hydrocortisone/dexamethasone, antenatal NSAIDs. Combined indomethacin + early steroids is synergistically toxic
- Other risks: chorioamnionitis, umbilical artery catheterisation, hypotension
- **Not** linked to feeding practices or formula (unlike NEC)
## Pathophysiology
Focal ischaemic necrosis of the antimesenteric bowel wall, most commonly at the terminal ileum near the ileocaecal valve. 
## Clinical features
- Sudden abdominal distension in an otherwise stable ELBW infant
- **Bluish/black abdominal wall discolouration** (classic; cf. erythema in NEC)
- Often less systemically toxic than perforated NEC at presentation
- Bloody stools uncommon
- Metabolic acidosis and hypotension develop after perforation

---
## Imaging
### AXR
- **Pneumoperitoneum** is often the **presenting** finding (cf. NEC where it is a late, advanced sign)
- Bowel gas pattern is otherwise **relatively normal** outside the free gas
- **No pneumatosis intestinalis**
- **No portal venous gas**
- Look for: football sign, falciform ligament sign, Rigler sign, triangle sign on supine film. Left lateral decubitus or cross-table lateral confirms
### US
- Free intraperitoneal fluid, often with debris/echogenic content
- May identify the perforation site or focal bowel wall thinning
- Bowel wall otherwise non-thickened, perfusion preserved away from perforation
---
## Management
- NPO, NG decompression, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics
- **Surgical**: primary peritoneal drainage (PPD) vs laparotomy
    - PPD often first choice in ELBW given haemodynamic instability
    - Laparotomy if no improvement after PPD or if clinical deterioration
- Outcomes generally **better than perforated NEC**: less bowel loss, lower short bowel syndrome risk, lower mortality

**Complications**: short bowel syndrome (less than NEC), strictures (rare), TPN cholestasis, neurodevelopmental delay (still significant given ELBW background).

> [!tip] Exam pearls
> 
> - **Classic vignette**: 26-week ELBW infant, day 5, received indomethacin for PDA, sudden abdominal distension with bluish abdominal wall, AXR shows pneumoperitoneum without pneumatosis → **SIP**, not NEC
> - **Pneumoperitoneum without pneumatosis** in an extreme preterm = think SIP
> - Distribution: SIP is focal at terminal ileum; NEC is more extensive and right-sided colonic involvement is common
> - Histology distinguishes: SIP has **no inflammation**, NEC has transmural inflammation with coagulative necrosis
> - PPD is more often used in SIP than perforated NEC

> [!warning] Common traps
> 
> - SIP is **not "early NEC"** or a mild form; treating it as such is conceptually and clinically wrong
> - Absence of pneumatosis in a perforated preterm does not rule out NEC outright (50-75% AXR sensitivity), but in the classic ELBW/indomethacin/day 5 context, SIP is the answer
> - PPD vs laparotomy choice is haemodynamic, not based on diagnosis alone
> - Do not call SIP on a term infant with pneumoperitoneum: think iatrogenic, volvulus, or meconium-related perforation instead
